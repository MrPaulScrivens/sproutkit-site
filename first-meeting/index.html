The room

Because you have been preparing for the wrong meeting

You are getting ready to listen well. To be organized, to be pleasant, to be the parent they want to help.

That instinct is sound and it is why most first meetings go the way they do. A parent who prepares to listen arrives with nothing in their hands. Everyone else at that table arrives with the file.

Eight people sit down. You know two of their names. The rest get introduced once, quickly, in the first ninety seconds while you are still finding a pen.

Then evaluation results. Then present levels. Then goals. Then minutes. Then placement. Then signatures, and the room stands up.

Your items come up in the middle if you raise them. At the end if you wait to be asked.

The two weeks

So the leverage is in the two weeks before the door opens

One email, sent about two weeks out, changes more than anything you could say on the day.

It asks for three things. Your child's education records, which you have the right to inspect and review under 34 CFR 300.501(a). The attendee list, which the notice was supposed to carry under 34 CFR 300.322(b). And the draft, which federal law does not require them to send you, and which most districts send anyway when asked.

That email moves the reading from the table to your kitchen.

A proposal read aloud at minute twenty is one you react to on the spot, in front of people, while somebody waits for your face to do something. At home you can mark it up and sleep on it.

On the day

And on the day, you hand something across the table

A room that has been handed paper stops and reads it. Everyone at that table has spent years being handed documents and the reflex is automatic.

The same information said out loud at minute forty gets nodded at and lost, because the person taking notes is already writing something else.

They have the file. You have the two hours after school. That is the only document in the room they cannot produce.

The difficult parent

But you do not want to be the difficult parent

Almost every parent going into a first meeting is carrying this one, and it does more damage than anything they misunderstand about the law. It is the reason people sign things.

So look at what is actually in here.

Asking for records. Asking who is coming. Asking someone to write down what they just promised. Asking what a word means.

The team writes IEPs for a living. Being asked to put something in the document is the most ordinary request you can make of them, and asking it eight times in one meeting is not rude.

None of it requires a raised voice or a clever line. You can say every sentence in here badly, in a shaky voice, reading off the page instead of looking at anyone, and it still does what it does.

What it will not do

It will not tell you the room is friendly.

Some of the people at that table will push hard for your child. Some are carrying forty other kids and a budget they did not set. On the day you will not be able to tell those two apart, and nothing in here will help you sort them.

What holds still is the regulation, the document, and the dates. What you asked for in writing, and what came back.

When the answer is no

The First Meeting covers one meeting, and it says so on its last page.
