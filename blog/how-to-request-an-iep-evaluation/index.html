How to Request an IEP Evaluation

August 5, 2026

You do not need permission to ask.

Either a parent or the district can start an initial evaluation, and the regulation names the parent first.

Consistent with the consent requirements in § 300.300, either a parent of a child or a public agency may initiate a request for an initial evaluation to determine if the child is a child with a disability.

That is 34 CFR 300.301(b).

Send it in writing

A conversation at pickup is not a request. It leaves no date and nothing to point at.

Email works. Email is better than paper, because the sent folder timestamps it for you.

Send it to the principal and the district’s special education director. If you do not know who that is, the district website lists them, usually under student services.

What goes in it

Four things, and it can be short.

Your child’s name, grade, and school.

That you are requesting an initial evaluation for special education under IDEA.

The specific areas you are worried about. Not “he is struggling.” Reading fluency. Attention during independent work. Handwriting. Whatever you actually see.

A request that they send the consent form.

That last line is the one most parents leave out, and it is the one that saves weeks.

Why the consent form matters more than the request

Your email does not start the clock.

The district sends a consent form. You sign it. The clock starts when they receive it back.

(1) (i) Must be conducted within 60 days of receiving parental consent for the evaluation; or (ii) If the State establishes a timeframe within which the evaluation must be conducted, within that timeframe;

That is 34 CFR 300.301(c)(1).

Nothing in the federal regulation puts a number on how long the district has to send you that form. So the days between your email and their consent form are days that belong to nobody, and they add up quietly.

Asking for the form by name in your first email closes that gap. It also makes the next follow-up easy, because you are chasing a specific document rather than a general answer.

Check your state’s number. 60 days is the federal floor. Some states run shorter, and some count school days rather than calendar days. Find your state’s timeframe before you cite a deadline out loud.

Name the areas before the testing starts

The evaluation has to cover every area connected to what you are worried about.

The child is assessed in all areas related to the suspected disability, including, if appropriate, health, vision, hearing, social and emotional status, general intelligence, academic performance, communicative status, and motor abilities;

That is 34 CFR 300.304(c)(4).

This is why the specific areas belong in the written request rather than in your head. When the results come back covering reading and saying nothing about attention, the record shows what you asked for.

What they can and cannot do

They can say no.

They cannot say no and leave you with nothing. A refusal to evaluate requires written notice, including what they considered and why they turned it down. 34 CFR 300.503.

If you get a verbal no, ask them to put it in writing. Use that phrasing. Ask for prior written notice of the refusal.

Nothing about that request is aggressive. It is the ordinary mechanism, and asking for it early is easier than reconstructing a conversation four months later.

After you send it

Put a reminder on your calendar for one week out.

If no consent form has arrived by then, reply to your own email and ask again. Replying to the original keeps the whole thread in one place, dated.

When the consent form does arrive, sign it and return it the same day if you can. That is the day your clock starts.
