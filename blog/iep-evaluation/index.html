The IEP Evaluation, Start to Finish

August 3, 2026

Somebody at the school said the word evaluation. Or you said it, and now you are waiting to find out what happens next.

Here is the whole process in order, with the regulation behind each step.

You can start it yourself

Most parents wait to be told their child will be tested. You do not have to wait.

Either a parent or the school district can request an initial evaluation. The regulation names the parent first.

Consistent with the consent requirements in § 300.300, either a parent of a child or a public agency may initiate a request for an initial evaluation to determine if the child is a child with a disability.

That is 34 CFR 300.301(b).

So “we will decide whether he needs testing” is not how the process works. You can put the request in writing today and the district has to respond to it.

Write it as an email. Date it. Keep the sent copy.

The clock starts at consent, not at your request

This is the part that costs parents months.

Your request does not start the 60 days. The district sends you a consent form, and the clock starts when you sign and return that form.

(1) (i) Must be conducted within 60 days of receiving parental consent for the evaluation; or (ii) If the State establishes a timeframe within which the evaluation must be conducted, within that timeframe;

That is 34 CFR 300.301(c)(1).

The gap between your email and that consent form is where time disappears. Nothing in the federal regulation puts a number on it.

So the practical move is to ask for the consent form by name. Not “please let me know the next steps.” Ask them to send the consent form for an initial evaluation.

Your state may have a different number. 60 days is the federal floor. Some states set a shorter clock and some count school days rather than calendar days, which is a real difference across a winter break. Check your own state’s timeframe before you say a deadline out loud in that room. Citing the wrong number hands the district an easy correction and costs you credibility for the rest of the meeting.

What the evaluation has to include

An evaluation is not one test.

In conducting the evaluation, the public agency must—(1) Use a variety of assessment tools and strategies to gather relevant functional, developmental, and academic information about the child, including information provided by the parent, that may assist in determining—(i) Whether the child is a child with a disability under § 300.8; and (ii) The content of the child’s IEP […] (2) Not use any single measure or assessment as the sole criterion for determining whether a child is a child with a disability and for determining an appropriate educational program for the child;

That is 34 CFR 300.304(b)(1)-(2).

Two things sit inside that language worth carrying into the meeting.

Information you provide counts as evaluation data. Work samples, the notes you kept, what you see at homework time. That is not a courtesy attachment to the real evaluation. The regulation lists it among the tools the district must use.

And no single measure decides it. If the whole case rests on one score, the evaluation is incomplete on its face.

It has to cover every area you are worried about

The child is assessed in all areas related to the suspected disability, including, if appropriate, health, vision, hearing, social and emotional status, general intelligence, academic performance, communicative status, and motor abilities;

That is 34 CFR 300.304(c)(4).

A reading evaluation that skips attention, when attention was half of why you asked, is not complete.

The move here happens before the testing starts, not after. Name the areas you want covered, in writing, when you send the request. Then you have a record of what was asked for to hold against what came back.

Asking again later

Once your child has an IEP, you can ask for a new evaluation. You do not need the district to agree the timing is right.

A public agency must ensure that a reevaluation of each child with a disability is conducted in accordance with §§ 300.304 through 300.311—(1) If the public agency determines that the educational or related services needs […] warrant a reevaluation; or (2) If the child’s parent or teacher requests a reevaluation.

That is 34 CFR 300.303(a).

A parent request is its own trigger, listed separately from the district’s own judgment. Put it in writing and date it.

When they say no

They can refuse. What they cannot do is refuse quietly.

If the district declines to evaluate, they owe you written notice of the refusal, including what they considered and why they turned it down. That request has a name and a number behind it. 34 CFR 300.503.

Ask for it in those words. A refusal that exists only as something somebody said in a room leaves you nothing to point at later.

What to do this week

If you have not asked yet, send the email. Name the areas you are worried about and ask them to send the consent form for an initial evaluation.

If you have asked and heard nothing, ask for the consent form by name again, in writing.

If you already signed consent, write the date on your calendar and count forward using your state’s timeframe.
