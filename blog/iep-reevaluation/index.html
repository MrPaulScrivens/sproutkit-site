Asking for a Reevaluation

August 10, 2026

Your child has an IEP. Something has changed, or something was never right, and the testing it was built on is old.

You can ask for new testing. You do not need the district to agree the timing is right.

The parent request is its own trigger

A public agency must ensure that a reevaluation of each child with a disability is conducted in accordance with §§ 300.304 through 300.311—(1) If the public agency determines that the educational or related services needs, including improved academic achievement and functional performance, of the child warrant a reevaluation; or (2) If the child’s parent or teacher requests a reevaluation.

That is 34 CFR 300.303(a).

Look at how the two clauses sit. The district’s own judgment is one trigger. A parent or teacher asking is a separate one, listed on its own.

So “we do not think he needs retesting yet” is an opinion about clause one, and clause two still stands on its own.

When it is worth asking

The three-year cycle is a floor, not a schedule you have to wait out.

Ask when the current testing no longer describes your child. A reading evaluation from second grade is not a description of a fifth grader.

Ask when something new has appeared. Anxiety that was not there before. A behavior pattern that started this year.

Ask when the goals keep coming back the same. Goals that repeat year over year usually mean the present levels underneath them are stale, and present levels come from evaluation data.

That last one is the veteran parent’s situation, and it is the one most often missed. The IEP reads fine. It reads fine because it says roughly what it said last year.

Send it the same way

In writing. Dated. To the case manager and the special education director.

Name what changed and what you want assessed. The evaluation has to cover every area connected to the suspected disability, so the areas you name are the areas that get looked at.

The child is assessed in all areas related to the suspected disability, including, if appropriate, health, vision, hearing, social and emotional status, general intelligence, academic performance, communicative status, and motor abilities;

That is 34 CFR 300.304(c)(4).

If anxiety is now part of the picture and you do not say so, social and emotional status may not be part of the reevaluation.

What they have to use

The same rules that governed the first evaluation govern this one.

In conducting the evaluation, the public agency must—(1) Use a variety of assessment tools and strategies to gather relevant functional, developmental, and academic information about the child, including information provided by the parent […] (2) Not use any single measure or assessment as the sole criterion […]

That is 34 CFR 300.304(b)(1)-(2).

What you send counts. Three years of report cards, the notes you kept, the private evaluation you paid for. That is evaluation data the team has to consider.

A reevaluation that consists of one updated score and a teacher checklist is thin, and the regulation says why.

When they say no

They can decline. The decline has to be written.

Prior written notice covers a refusal to act, not only a proposal to act. If the district refuses to reevaluate, ask for that refusal in writing, including what they considered and why. 34 CFR 300.503.

A no you can read is different from a no you remember. The first one is a document. The second one is your word against theirs at a meeting eight months from now.

What to do this week

Find the date of the last evaluation. It is in the IEP, usually in the first few pages.

Write down what has changed since then, in plain sentences.

Send the request naming those areas, and ask for the consent form.
