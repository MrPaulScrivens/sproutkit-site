The IEP Evaluation Timeline

August 7, 2026

The number most parents have heard is 60 days. It is a real number and it is often the wrong one to be counting.

What the 60 days actually measures

(1) (i) Must be conducted within 60 days of receiving parental consent for the evaluation; or (ii) If the State establishes a timeframe within which the evaluation must be conducted, within that timeframe;

That is 34 CFR 300.301(c)(1).

Read the trigger. The count runs from the district receiving your signed consent, which is a later date than the day you asked.

Two dates, and only the second one starts anything.

Where the time goes

You email in September asking for an evaluation.

The consent form arrives in October. You sign it that week.

The evaluation is due 60 days after that, which lands in December, and with a winter break inside it the meeting happens in January.

Four months, and the district was never late. The federal regulation puts no deadline on the stretch between your request and their consent form.

That gap is the part you can actually affect, and the way to affect it is to ask for the consent form by name in your first email. Not the next steps. The consent form for an initial evaluation.

Your state’s number probably differs

60 days is the federal floor. The regulation says so in its own text, in that second clause about a state establishing a timeframe.

Two things vary by state.

The count itself. Some states run shorter than 60.

And what a day means. Some states count school days rather than calendar days. Across a district with a two week winter break, that is a substantial difference in the same number.

Look up your state’s timeframe before you cite a deadline in a meeting. A wrong number gets corrected in the room, and the correction costs you more than the deadline gained you.

Your Parent Training and Information Center will have your state’s number, and they are free.

What happens at the end of it

The evaluation gets done and the team meets to decide eligibility.

That meeting is where the results get explained and where a decision gets made about whether your child qualifies. If they do, an IEP gets written.

Ask for the evaluation report before that meeting rather than at it. Reading eleven pages of testing while eight people wait for you to react is not reading.

Nothing in the federal regulation requires them to send it in advance. Districts often will if asked, and the ask costs nothing.

If the deadline passes

First, check the date you are counting from. In most cases where a parent believes the district blew the deadline, the count started at the request rather than at consent.

If the date really has passed, put it in writing. One line noting the consent date and the days elapsed, asking when the evaluation will be complete.

Written, dated, and specific. That is the whole move.

What to do this week

Find your consent date. It is on the form you signed, and the district has a copy.

Look up your state’s timeframe.

Count forward from consent and put that date on your calendar.
